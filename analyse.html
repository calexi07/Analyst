1 · How to read this report

Every currency gets a score from −18 to +18. Think of it as a health check of the currency's economic support. In practice most scores are between −6 and +6.

−3 or lower
Bearish
fundamentals point down
−2 to +2
Neutral
no clear direction
+3 or higher
Bullish
fundamentals point up

How the score is made. Six "ingredients" are each rated from −2 to +2 (see section 5). Each has a weight; the most important is "surprise potential in rate expectations" (35%). Score = 9 × weighted sum. To avoid jumping around, the score only changes if the new number differs from last week's by at least 1 point.

Three labels next to each score:

  • Bias — the direction (bullish / neutral / bearish).
  • Conviction — how strong the signal is: Low (weak), Medium (moderate), High (strong).
  • Confidence — how good the data behind it are (High = mostly official or reliable sources). It is not "chance of being right".

Pairs. For a pair like EURUSD, take the first currency's score minus the second's: −4 − (+2) = −6. Gap +3 or more = bullish pair (fundamentals favour the first currency, the pair tends to rise). Gap −3 or less = bearish pair (favour the second currency, the pair tends to fall). Between −2 and +2 = two-sided (no clear direction).

5 · The six ingredients

Each ingredient is rated −2, −1, 0, +1 or +2 (shown as −−, −, 0, +, ++).

R1 · Interest-rate level ("carry") weight 15%

How high this country's interest rate is expected to be in about 6 months, ranked against the other seven. A higher rate pays investors more for holding the currency, so it attracts money. Rank 1 = +2, rank 8 = −2.

R2 · Surprise potential in rate expectations weight 35%

The most important ingredient. Question: will the market have to change its mind about this central bank? + if the bank's words AND recent data suggest more hikes than the market expects; − if they suggest fewer. 0 if the market already expects the right thing.

R3 · Recent data surprises weight 10%

Did important data or central-bank decisions since last week come in stronger (+) or weaker (−) than expected? Half of last week's value carries over.

N1 · Commodity prices ("terms of trade") weight 10%

Price change over 4 weeks of what the country sells or buys: oil for CAD (+ when oil rises), iron ore for AUD, dairy for NZD. EUR and JPY import oil, so a big oil rise is − for them. USD, GBP and CHF are always 0.

N2 · Political, budget, trade and intervention risk weight 15%

− when there is a dated risky event (a vote, a rating review) and markets show stress, or a live tariff threat. + when a risk eases or the government threatens to defend its currency.

G1 · Global risk mood × currency type weight 15%

First the 'risk mood' is measured from US stocks, the VIX fear index and credit spreads. Then it is multiplied by the currency type: AUD/NZD like good moods; JPY/CHF (safe havens) like bad moods. This week the mood is neutral (0), so this ingredient is 0 for everyone.

16 · Glossary

Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of a percent. 25bp = 0.25%.
Bias
The direction the fundamentals point to: bullish (score +3 or more), bearish (−3 or less), neutral (between −2 and +2). For a pair: bullish = fundamentals favour the first currency, bearish = favour the second, two-sided = no clear winner.
Breakeven inflation
What bond markets expect US inflation to be over the next 10 years. Rising breakevens usually help gold.
Bullish / bearish
Bullish = fundamentals point up (stronger). Bearish = fundamentals point down (weaker). This is NOT a trade signal.
Carry
The interest you earn by holding a currency. High-rate currencies (AUD now) have high carry; CHF at 0% has none.
Censure / 49.3
Censure = parliament votes to remove the French government. 49.3 = article of the French constitution that lets the government pass a budget without a vote, which often triggers a censure motion.
Central bank
The institution that sets a country's interest rate (Fed, ECB, BoE, BoJ, SNB, BoC, RBA, RBNZ).
Confidence
How good the evidence behind a score is (official data and reliable sources = High). It is about data quality, not a probability of being right.
Consensus
The average forecast of economists for a data release. A 'surprise' is the difference between the actual figure and consensus.
Conviction
How strong the signal is. Low = weak, Medium = moderate, High = strong (needs a big score, agreement between parts of the model, and good evidence). No currency or pair is High this week.
CPI / HICP / PCE
Measures of inflation (how fast prices rise). HICP is the euro-area version; PCE is the one the Fed prefers.
Final check (delta check)
A short last check before Monday's open. Scores change only for new news after the cutoff or a missed fact, by at most one step per ingredient and 2 points per currency. Price moves never change a score.
Dissent
A central-bank member who voted differently from the decision.
Dovish / hawkish
Dovish = wants lower interest rates or fewer hikes (usually bad for the currency). Hawkish = wants higher rates or more hikes (usually good for the currency).
ETF flows (gold)
Money going into (inflows) or out of (outflows) funds that hold physical gold.
Fundamentals
The economic reasons behind a currency's value: interest rates, inflation, growth, politics, commodities. Not charts.
Gap (pair)
First currency's score minus second currency's score. Example: EURUSD = −4 − (+2) = −6.
GDP
The total value of what a country produces; the main measure of growth.
GDT
Global Dairy Trade, an auction that sets world dairy prices.
Grade A/B/C/D
Quality of a source. A = official (central bank, statistics office, exchange pricing tool). B = reliable news quoting the figure. C = single broker comment or older data. D = missing; the ingredient is then set to 0.
Haven (safe haven)
An asset people buy when they are afraid: traditionally JPY, CHF and gold.
Hike / hold / cut
Raise / keep / lower the interest rate.
Hysteresis
A stability rule: the published score changes only if the new calculation differs from last week's score by 1 point or more. It stops scores flipping on tiny changes.
ISM / PMI
Surveys of company managers. Above 50 = business growing, below 50 = shrinking.
Market pricing / "priced"
What traders already expect, measured from futures and swaps. '81% priced' = the market sees about an 81% chance of that move.
Payrolls (NFP)
Number of jobs added in the US last month; the most watched US data.
Priced-in
Largely priced = the good or bad news is already expected by the market, so less room for a surprise. Partially priced = some room left for the market to move. Not applicable = no clear market pricing to compare with.
Raw score
The score straight from the formula (9 × weighted sum of the six ingredients), before rounding and the hysteresis rule.
Real yield
The interest rate on a bond minus expected inflation. When real yields fall, gold usually benefits (holding gold costs less).
Risk mood (regime)
Is the market calm and optimistic (risk-on) or afraid (risk-off)? Measured from the S&P 500, the VIX and US credit spreads. This week: neutral.
Score
From −18 (very weak fundamentals) to +18 (very strong). Most scores sit between −6 and +6.
Spread (OAT–Bund)
How much more France pays to borrow than Germany. Wider = more worry about France.
State
Strong (score +5 or more), Weak (−5 or less), Improving (up 2 or more vs last week), Weakening (down 2 or more), Neutral (otherwise).
Swing horizon
The time frame of this analysis: several days to several weeks.
Tariff
A tax on imports. US tariffs on Canada hurt CAD.
Two-sided
A pair whose two currencies score within 2 points of each other: no clear fundamental direction.
VIX
The 'fear index' of the US stock market. Below 15 = calm, above 25 = fear. Now 19.4 (normal).
